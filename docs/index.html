Research

Research Interests

I study the economics of information and digital technologies. My current work spans two related threads. The AI divide asks whether generative AI is a force for socioeconomic mobility or stratification: how people across demographic lines differ in adopting AI tools, in engaging with them, and in the welfare they ultimately gain. AI and information ecosystems examines how generative AI is reshaping the way people seek and consume information, as people and AI systems adapt to one another. This mutual adaptation changes what content gets produced, how diverse and reliable it is, and which publishers and creators get discovered on the open web.

I’m also broadly interested in how political ideology and social norms govern the production and consumption of cultural goods in an increasingly polarized world.

To study these questions, I combine economic theory with computational methods, including causal inference, machine learning, and natural language processing, to measure and estimate effects in large-scale structured and unstructured data.

Working Papers

Answering Without Referring: How AI Search Rewrites the Web’s Economic Bargain

Qiaoni Shi, Kai Zhu, and Kai Gu

ISMS Marketing Science Conference Lisbon June 2026
Bocconi Marketing Research Camp May 2026

Search engines have long allocated attention on the web by routing users from queries to websites. AI search changes this arrangement because information needs can be resolved inside the intermediary. Using URL-level Comscore U.S. desktop clickstream, we compare ChatGPT and Google information-seeking occasions and exploit ChatGPT Search access expansions to estimate traditional search displacement. ChatGPT produces outbound clicks in only 5.2% of conversation sessions, far below Google’s referral ratio. The remaining clicks are not a scaled-down Google stream: they skew toward specialized destinations and away from ad-supported sites. Wider access cuts search use by 9.4%, with search-referral losses largest for informational categories. Our findings identify a central economic shift in digital intermediation: AI search might satisfy information needs inside the intermediary while weakening the referral bargain that has linked search, traffic, and content production on the open web.
