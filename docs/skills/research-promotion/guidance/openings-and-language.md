# Standfirsts, opening paragraphs, and language

Use this guide to compose sentences, not merely select a story outline. Its
patterns and word ranges are editorial recommendations, not universal rules or
claims about readership. Worked copy is original and labeled synthetic. Source
identities are in the [catalog](../references/sources.md); the separate
[opening audit](../audits/openings-audit.md) records the observations behind the
patterns and is needed only for provenance questions or maintenance.

## Give the three entry points separate jobs

| Element | Reader's question | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | Why open this? | The specific puzzle, consequence, or tension; economical enough to grasp at a glance |
| Standfirst | What will I learn? | The central insight and a reason it matters, with enough context to understand it |
| Lead and following paragraphs | How do I enter this story, and why believe it? | A situation or evidence problem, followed by a clear bridge to what the study establishes |

Some central facts will recur because these elements can be read separately. Give
the recurrence a new function: the standfirst summarizes the result, the body
explains the decision behind it or supplies its evidence. Do not write the same
summary three times with different synonyms.

## Write a standfirst that reveals something

For an ordinary university story, start with one sentence of about 25–40 words;
use two short sentences when the insight benefits from a second beat.
This is a drafting target, not a length to fill. Avoid a mini-abstract containing
background, sample, estimator, results, contribution, and future research.

Choose the sentence's main subject and verb before adding credentials. Usually
the subject is the affected people, institution, technology, or intervention. A
study-led sentence is useful when the evidence itself is newsworthy, but the reader
still needs to learn what it found.

Three useful constructions, to vary rather than copy mechanically:

- **Result and scope:** In a named setting or design, an action changed a specified
  outcome, with a concrete consequence for an affected group. Use a causal verb
  only if the design supports it.
- **Hidden mechanism:** An everyday tool influences a decision through a less
  obvious action. Identify the mechanism's evidence status if it is only proposed.
- **Tradeoff or boundary:** An apparent benefit comes with a supported cost, or an
  effect appears for one outcome while another remains unresolved. Name both
  outcomes rather than promising unspecified unintended consequences.

Put a salient number here if it is the story. Otherwise use the space for meaning
and give the number in the body. Include design labels such as “model” or “survey”
when they prevent readers from mistaking prediction or association for observation
or causation. Usually save author lists, affiliation strings, and journal prestige
for the paragraph that introduces the research.

A standfirst does not need a limitations sentence by default. Its wording should
make the claim accurate in the first place. Keep the measured outcome recognizable:
waiting time is not total visit time, and parcels are not emissions. Mention a
missing measure here only when readers would otherwise misunderstand the claim;
other reporting gaps can wait for the relevant body section or editorial note.

**Synthetic edit:** “New research explores the complex relationship between defaults
and sustainable consumption” says neither what happened nor what was measured.
For the delivery experiment below: “At one online retailer, making combined delivery
the default led shoppers to choose it more often and reduced parcels per order.”
The revision names the action and result. Because it makes no emissions claim,
an emissions disclaimer would interrupt the story without clarifying it.

**Standfirst check:** Cover the headline. Does the sentence still identify the subject
and deliver a finding? Then read both together. Does the standfirst add information?
If it could sit under ten unrelated papers, replace general nouns with the actual
actors, choice, and result.

## Build the opening paragraphs

Choose one route, informed by the article's actual evidence. Three paragraphs are
a useful planning unit, not a required layout. In a short story, combine them. As
a starting point, aim for roughly 40–70 words in the first paragraph and reach the
study and its answer within the first 150–220 body words. Never pad to meet these
ranges; move the answer sooner when a direct news lead works better.

Plan what the reader learns in each paragraph before drafting. A third paragraph
can explain a comparison, introduce a mechanism, distinguish affected groups, or
develop a practical tradeoff. It is not a default slot for the study's complete
limitations list. Keep the boundary that changes the opening's meaning; defer
unrelated unmeasured outcomes until they become relevant.

### Route A: a system, then a concrete change

Adapt the Goodreads progression for a documented policy change or institutional
event.

- **Paragraph 1:** Name who controls a consequential choice. Show what that choice
  affects in two or three concrete examples. End with the specific pressure or
  decision that creates this story's problem.
- **Paragraph 2:** Name the case. Establish how things worked before, identify what
  changed and when, then expose the result or question that follows. A date belongs
  here when it marks a real turning point.
- **Paragraph 3:** Introduce the research team and the evidence that lets the story
  go beyond anecdote. Give the principal answer, then move to the affected actors.

Avoid an opening about how technology is transforming the world. The broad frame
must contain the actual action the case will explain. Do not use “as a result” for
a sequence the study can establish only as “after.”

### Route B: an everyday situation, then its hidden logic

Adapt the framing-questions essay for theory or a mechanism that readers can picture.

- **Paragraph 1:** Invite the reader into an explicitly hypothetical choice. Use two
  or three ordinary details that correspond to the model's actors or constraints.
  End with the puzzle in that situation, not a fabricated emotional reaction.
- **Paragraph 2:** Translate the scene into the real research problem. Identify who
  wants what, what they do not know, or where their incentives differ. Introduce
  the model or study and its central insight in everyday language.
- **Paragraph 3:** Show why the result follows, using the same actors. State the
  important condition where it becomes relevant. Reserve formal definitions for
  terms readers actually need.

The scene earns its space only if it explains the research. Do not invent a named
participant, interview, or observed encounter. For theory, keep the fact that this
is a model near the result, then explain the decision rather than repeatedly
announcing that it is not an experiment.

### Route C: an apparent answer, then the unresolved comparison

Adapt the X-algorithm article or the Economist's evidence-revision opening for a
question on which the available evidence genuinely leaves a puzzle.

- **Paragraph 1:** State a specific public question or documented belief. Explain
  what an earlier comparison did or did not show. End on the new comparison that
  could change the answer.
- **Paragraph 2:** Introduce the study through what researchers compared or changed,
  then identify the researchers and publication status without a long roll call.
- **Paragraph 3:** Deliver the answer directly. A short sentence can carry the turn;
  the next sentence gives magnitude, scope, or a consequential exception.

A research gap alone is not a public puzzle. Explain why the two comparisons
could lead a reader to think differently. Do not invent a popular consensus to
manufacture a reversal. If no prior belief or comparison is documented, lead
directly with the new result and explain the decision it bears on.

## Keep an engaging register

For the full word-to-paragraph editing framework, use [Prose editing](prose-editing.md).
Its [reference audit](../audits/prose-audit.md) examines the body and ending as well as the
opening. The guidance below concerns the additional voice choices a public-facing
lead can make.

Questions should open a solvable puzzle. Answer them promptly. Second person works
well for an imagined choice; it should not presume the reader's beliefs, feelings,
or behavior. Warmth can come from recognition and precise detail without an invented
author confession or forced excitement.

Use one coherent metaphor if it clarifies the mechanism. Specify its literal
meaning nearby: a “gate” can stand for a rule controlling participation. Avoid
combining gates, dominoes, waves, and battlefields in the same explanation. Ordinary
words can be vivid without figurative language. Cut generic intensifiers such as
groundbreaking, revolutionary, fascinating, or profoundly when the next sentence
already contains the evidence of importance.

## Worked opening: an empirical story

**Synthetic source brief:** A six-week randomized trial at one online retailer
assigned 3,600 shoppers to either combined delivery as the checkout default or
an active choice with no option preselected. Both groups could choose either
delivery option. Combined-delivery uptake was 60% versus 45%; mean parcels per
order were 1.7 versus 2.0. The study found no detectable change in order completion.
Emissions and longer-term outcomes were not measured. No author details are supplied.

**Headline:** One checkout default, fewer parcels

**Standfirst:** In a six-week trial at one online retailer, shoppers chose combined
delivery more often when it was preselected. That small checkout change reduced
the number of parcels sent per order.

**Opening paragraph 1 — the decision:** The final choice at checkout can determine
how many parcels arrive at the door. Shoppers can have purchases sent together or
separately. A retailer also makes a choice: whether to leave that decision entirely
open or give customers a starting option they can change.

**Opening paragraph 2 — the answer:** In an experiment involving 3,600 shoppers,
combined delivery was chosen by 60% of those who saw it preselected, compared with
45% of those asked to make an active choice. Both groups could select either option.
The default changed the outcome even though the options remained available.

**Opening paragraph 3 — the consequence:** Random assignment allowed the researchers
to test the effect of the checkout design. Orders generated 1.7 parcels on average
with the default, compared with 2.0 in the comparison group. The retailer changed
how the choice was presented, and shoppers' decisions changed what it shipped.

**Why it works:** The standfirst supplies the finding, setting, and duration. The lead
introduces a decision readers can picture. The next paragraphs answer the question
and establish its support. The copy does not attribute shoppers' choices to laziness,
claim that everyone prefers combined delivery, or assert measured emissions savings.
Real author and paper details would enter with the experiment when available.

## Worked opening: a theory story

**Synthetic source brief:** A model studies a travel-booking adviser that chooses
the order of questions about price and flexibility. It does not know the customer's
preferences and seeks booking commission. Under specified payoff and information
assumptions, a randomized question policy achieves a higher worst-case commission
than every deterministic policy. No user experiment or welfare result is supplied.

**Headline:** Why a booking assistant might vary its questions

**Standfirst:** A model of travel booking finds that varying how an adviser asks
about price and flexibility can raise the minimum commission it secures when
customers' priorities are unknown.

**Opening paragraph 1:** Imagine a booking assistant asking what matters most:
a cheaper ticket or the freedom to change your plans. It must decide which question
to ask first without knowing your priorities. It also has an objective of its own:
earning a commission on the booking.

**Opening paragraph 2:** A model of that decision finds an advantage in varying
the questions. A randomized questioning policy secures a higher commission in the
least favorable case than any policy that never randomizes. The insight concerns how the adviser
protects its earnings when it has to ask before it knows what the customer wants.

**Why it works:** The imagined choice provides the actors and information problem.
The bridge identifies the model and whose payoff improves, then develops the insight.
There is no invented test of travelers, claim about better advice, or sequence of
paragraphs defining mathematical terms before readers know the decision at stake.

## Revise the opening as a unit

Read the headline, standfirst, and first three paragraphs together. Cut a sentence
if it only announces importance, repeats the result without adding explanation,
or repeats a boundary that is already clear. Replace an interchangeable first
sentence with the actual actor and choice. Check that the paragraph introducing
the study answers the question raised by the lead. Finally, read just the opening
claim: it should give readers the right central idea, with detail developed in the story.
