# Prose editing at five levels

Use for substantive drafting or revision of research news, features, and social
copy. Apply the relevant decisions; do not display a twenty-point checklist before
giving the user their draft. The [reference audit](../audits/prose-audit.md) records source
observations, counterexamples, access limits, and the evidence behind the decision
IDs below. These are editorial inferences, not proven engagement tactics.

Keep the public-facing purpose: an intelligible, interesting story with an accurate
payoff. Do not import academic density in pursuit of precision, or remove necessary
distinctions in pursuit of simplicity. Use [the opening guide](openings-and-language.md)
for headline, standfirst, and lead architecture; this guide applies throughout.

## 1. Words: name the right thing economically

**W1 — Preserve meaning before smoothing wording.** Keep the actor, action, outcome,
comparison, unit, and scope stable. A more familiar word is helpful only if it
means the same thing here. Do not substitute sales for purchase incidence, total
processing time for queue time, or political identity for an issue attitude.
Precision can require repeating the same noun rather than finding a synonym.
Recheck headlines and practical takeaways against the outcome label: the numerical
sentence can be right while its summary quietly changes what was measured.

**W2 — Choose what to do with technical vocabulary.** Keep a term when the distinction
is central, readers will encounter it again, or translating it would become longer
or less exact. Give its contextual meaning at first consequential use. Translate a
term that only labels a familiar action; omit one that adds credentials but no
explanatory value. A natural experiment needs its actual comparison explained;
naming the design alone does not tell a public reader why it is informative.

For an unfamiliar concept, use **concrete instance → plain explanation → term**
when the example can establish its meaning. Use **term → immediate gloss** when
the name is itself the topic. Do not force the first pattern onto a story whose
reader already knows the concept. In a short post, selecting a narrower insight
may work better than packing in multiple definitions.

**W3 — Let verbs reveal action.** Where a noun hides a process, identify who does
what: “the introduction of charges” can become “the platform began charging.”
Keep verbs of evidence calibrated separately: an observed association does not
become an effect because the sentence now has an active subject. Passive voice is
useful when the affected people or comparison deserve the first position, as in
“Applicants were randomly assigned.” Avoid inventing an agent merely to make it active.

**W4 — Keep useful abstract nouns.** Retain a noun that names an outcome, mechanism,
constraint, or previously explained relationship. Participation, uncertainty, and
coordination costs can provide stable subjects around which an explanation grows.
Connect them to observable actions or a concrete example before asking readers to
reason with them. Replace abstract noun stacks that neither specify an action nor
support later reasoning. The problem is an unexplained abstraction, not abstraction
itself. A useful test: can the reader say what this noun refers to in this story?

**W5 — Make abbreviations earn their introduction.** Consider reader familiarity,
how often the short form recurs, and whether a plain short label would work.
Introducing an unfamiliar acronym for one later mention usually costs more than
it saves. Spell out a necessary unfamiliar abbreviation at first use; a familiar
one such as AI may need no expansion for the intended audience. Do not expand an
unnecessary acronym into an equally unnecessary technical name: remove the naming
burden. Prefer “1,200 applicants” to sample-size notation in general-interest copy.

## 2. Sentences: order information and make relationships visible

**S1 — Start from something the reader can place.** After the first sentence,
connect the opening phrase to an actor, question, or finding already available.
Then add the new action or consequence. “Those new accounts…” can carry the reader
from following behavior into later exposure. An explicit noun may be clearer than
“this,” especially after a sentence with several possible referents. Put a new
setting or time first when it is needed for orientation; do not apply given-to-new
order mechanically to every lead or purposeful surprise.

**S2 — Put emphasis where the meaning belongs.** Let the main clause state the
sentence's principal claim. Use the end for a consequential new detail, comparison,
or answer. Move author credentials and incidental dates away from that position
unless they are the news. Do not bury the finding inside a parenthesis while the
main clause merely announces a publication. Sometimes a short sentence deserves
the conclusion; do not add one when it only repeats the previous sentence.

**S3 — Make comparisons parallel in grammar and substance.** Match what each side
describes: population, outcome, unit, period, and analysis basis. “68% renewed on
time with the revised notice, compared with 61% with the standard notice” gives
readers one dimension to compare. A result for all participants cannot silently
be compared with one for completers. Repeated syntax helps list comparable findings;
it does not make different measures comparable. Explain genuine differences rather
than forcing symmetry. Distinguish relative percentages from percentage points.

**S4 — Choose punctuation for its job.** Use a colon after a complete lead-in to
deliver an explanation, answer, or list. Use a semicolon for closely related
independent clauses when their balance matters. Parentheses suit optional context;
essential limits belong in the sentence's main flow. A dash can mark an interruption
or turn, but several nested insertions separate subjects from verbs and comparisons
from their counterparts. Split or reorder such a sentence before adding another
punctuation layer. Punctuation can expose a relationship, not supply a missing one.

**S5 — Preserve the force of supported claims.** Before weakening a sentence or
adding a qualification, identify the likely, material misunderstanding it prevents.
If none can be named, retain the stronger accurate wording. When clarification is
needed, prefer a precise subject, verb, comparison, or time frame to an additional
disclaimer. Trust context already established. Words such as “may,” “suggests,”
and “observed” should express a real distinction, not habitual caution. When a
null finding matters, name the outcome precisely.

## 3. Length variation: integrate reasoning, then orient

**L1 — Keep a longer sentence when the clauses belong together.** A reason, condition,
comparison, and result may be easier to understand as one grammatical relationship
than as four disconnected assertions. For example, a leading “because” clause can
explain why the main clause follows; an “although” clause can preserve the contrast.
Keep the main subject and verb easy to locate. Long lists of affiliations or parenthetic
definitions are different from integrated reasoning.

**L2 — Use short sentences to orient, answer, or turn.** After a complex comparison,
a short sentence can establish which interpretation matters. It should contribute
a consequence or clarification, not merely say the result is surprising. A brief
question can announce the problem that the next passage resolves. Avoid sequences
of fragments that make the reader reconstruct the missing logical links.

**L3 — Split by conceptual load.** Split when a sentence changes actors, introduces
a second independent finding, or nests a qualification inside an already unfamiliar
comparison. Keep connected clauses together when splitting would hide the condition
or causal relationship. Read the passage aloud or inspect clause structure; do not
alternate long and short sentences to a fixed pattern or optimize an average length.

## 4. Paragraphs: one governing message, several supporting claims

**P1 — Identify the paragraph's governing message.** Privately summarize it in a
plain sentence. Every other sentence should supply evidence, explanation, an example,
a boundary, or a consequence of that message. One paragraph can contain several
claims if they do this work. Its opening often gives the message, but a brief scene
or accumulating explanation can earn a later conclusion.

**P2 — Split when the reader's question changes.** A result, an explanation of its
mechanism, and a new policy recommendation may require different paragraphs. Split
where a new governing message begins, not after a fixed number of sentences. Keep
an essential limitation with its finding, even if a visual break would look cleaner.
Move an unrelated credit list or call to action out of a tightly reasoned paragraph.

**P3 — Combine fragments that do one job.** Adjacent one-line paragraphs may each
be grammatical yet force needless stops between a claim, its evidence, and its
meaning. Combine them when they answer one question and need each other. Preserve
a short standalone paragraph when it makes a real turn or gives a consequential
answer. Screen readability permits breathing room; it does not require every
sentence to become its own paragraph.

**P4 — Develop a missing step.** Add explanation when a term is unfamiliar, a number
has no interpretable comparison, a proposed mechanism skips a link, or an implication
is merely announced. Use evidence already available. If the source cannot support
the step, narrow or remove the conclusion and note the gap outside the copy when
needed. Do not add another restatement of the topic sentence to make a thin paragraph
look developed.

## 5. Pacing: manage the reader's work

**T1 — Audit information density separately from length.** Notice where unfamiliar
terms, new actors, statistics, and conditions arrive together. Several short sentences
can still be dense. After a demanding comparison, provide its meaning or a concrete
example before introducing a second one.

Use as many numbers as the story needs and the format supports; there is no fixed
quota. Each should establish a magnitude, supply a comparison, reveal variation,
or explain a consequential pattern. Include the units, baseline, time frame, and
uncertainty needed to interpret it. Remove numbers that duplicate an established
point or introduce unused detail. Manage density through sequencing, explanation,
and paragraph structure. For a short post, choose fewer claims when needed.

**T2 — Make transitions state a relationship.** Indicate sequence, contrast, reason,
new affected group, or change of scale. Reusing a clear subject can be enough.
“However” requires a real contrast; “therefore” requires an inference the evidence
supports. Do not repair an incoherent sequence by adding moreover, importantly,
or another generic signpost. Reorder the ideas instead.

**T3 — Put breaks at useful pauses.** Break after a resolved comparison, before a
new reader question, or when the story changes scale from a case to a wider issue.
Use a subheading for a substantive stage of a long story, not for every paragraph.
On social platforms, preserve complete units of thought even when making blocks
shorter. A thread post should add a new step; it need not correspond to exactly
one paragraph in the source article.

**T4 — Repeat with a purpose.** Repeat a technical or outcome label to keep the
referent stable. Return to an opening example after the explanation so the reader
can understand it differently. Restate a finding when it acquires a new job, such
as moving from headline to evidence or from evidence to implication. Cut repetition
that adds no evidence, interpretation, connection, or emphasis. Avoid elegant
synonyms that make one construct appear to be several different outcomes.

## Worked revision across the five levels

**Synthetic evidence; not a real study.** In an eight-week randomized trial at one
licensing office, 1,200 renewal notices used either a highlighted deadline or the
standard layout. On-time renewal was 68% versus 61%, a 7-percentage-point difference.
The trial found no detectable difference in the fee paid per renewed permit. Why
the formatting worked was not established; applicant time costs were not measured.
Assume no author names, paper link, or quotation have been supplied.

**Flawed teaching draft:**

> A novel RCT concerning the optimization of temporal information salience involved
> the randomized allocation of 1,200 renewal notices over an eight-week period to
> the highlighted-deadline condition or the standard-layout condition. An OTR of
> 68% was observed in the former, while the latter was 61%. This represents a 7%
> improvement, which is significant for administrative efficiency and demonstrates
> that recipients paid more attention. There was no fee effect. The results have
> important practical implications.

**Revised story passage:**

Highlighting a deadline helped more people renew their permits on time. In an
eight-week randomized trial at one licensing office, 68% of notices with the
highlighted deadline led to on-time renewal, compared with 61% of standard notices.
That is a gain of 7 percentage points.

The comparison came from 1,200 notices randomly assigned to the two layouts. Because
the researchers assigned the layout rather than letting recipients choose it, they
could test the effect of the formatting on timely renewal.

The result gives licensing offices a practical reason to look at how they present
a deadline. The date is already on the notice; making it stand out can change
whether people act on time.

**What the revision does:**

- **Words:** Removes two unnecessary abbreviations and a noun stack. Retains renewal
  as a useful outcome label. Corrects percentage points and drops an unsupported
  attention mechanism and efficiency claim.
- **Sentences:** Gives the two groups parallel measures. Puts the result before
  incidental design detail and expresses the practical implication directly.
- **Length:** A longer comparison integrates conditions and rates; shorter sentences
  interpret the difference and focus attention on the decision.
- **Paragraphs:** The first supports a finding, the second explains the comparison,
  and the third develops the practical implication. Several claims serve each message.
- **Pacing:** The draft moves from result to support to interpretation. Repeated
  renewal/layout terms maintain continuity; the vague importance sentence disappears.

This is one useful arrangement, not a template. The passage omits fees, time costs,
and the unknown mechanism because none is needed for its point. It draws a broader
practical lesson without claiming that other offices have already reproduced the
result. A shorter social post could combine the finding and comparison.

## Apply the guide without flattening the voice

Preserve successful passages, intended emphasis, and the author's purpose. Revise
to solve an identifiable communication problem; added caution, abstraction, or
methodological detail is not inherently an improvement.

Work from story order and paragraph messages to sentences and words, then density,
rhythm, and continuity. Recheck meaning after cuts. The reader should encounter a
developing account of what was learned and why it matters. If the story repeatedly
pauses to explain what the research cannot say, reconsider its wording and structure.
