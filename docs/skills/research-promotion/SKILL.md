# Research Promotion

Make a reader care about what the research changes. Write with curiosity, concrete
stakes, rhythm, and a satisfying payoff.

**Governing rule: Tell the story with confidence. Lead with the insight, develop
its meaning, and trust readers to understand ordinary context. Use vivid language,
selective detail, and reasoned implications freely. Add an explicit qualification
only to correct a likely, material misunderstanding; otherwise let accurate wording
and the surrounding story carry the scope.**

Accuracy means faithful claims, comparisons, and attribution, not an inventory of
robustness checks or anticipated objections. Develop implications, economic
consequences, and broader questions through explicit reasoning. Keep facts,
numbers, and quotations faithful, and distinguish interpretation from measured results.

A public-facing story has a different sequence from a paper. Begin with a decision,
puzzle, consequence, or familiar experience; introduce the study when it helps
answer the reader's question. A simplified abstract is rarely the right product.

## Set the assignment from the available context

Read the supplied paper, abstract, brief, or draft before asking questions. Infer
the audience, channel, language, speaker, and desired length where possible.
Author voice can use “we”; institutional and independent editorial voices should
identify the researchers. Do not invent the author's feelings or personal history.

Deliver the requested format only. For an unspecified request to promote a paper,
default to a general-interest LinkedIn draft and one short X post, and state that
choice briefly. For a requested full package, cover the named channels. Treat
“a thread” as a connected post sequence unless context indicates Meta Threads.

Ask only for missing information that changes the substance: most importantly, a
paper or usable research brief when neither is available. An abstract can support
a scoped draft; identify that source limit outside the copy rather than pretending
to have read the methods or inventing detail. Respect embargoes supplied by the user.

## Establish what can be said

For a full paper, read beyond the abstract: inspect findings, methods, relevant
tables or propositions, and limitations. Keep a compact working record; a short
post does not require a displayed evidence table.

- **Identity:** title, authors, version/date, publication status, and paper URL/DOI
  when supplied or verified. Distinguish a working paper from an accepted article.
- **Findings:** the results relevant to the story in ordinary language; population,
  setting, time, comparison, units, magnitude, and uncertainty that affect interpretation.
- **Support:** a page, table, figure, section, proposition, or supplied-brief locator
  for each central claim and number. Resolve conflicts in versions before using them.
- **Design:** randomized intervention, observational comparison, model, review,
  interviews, or other approach, and what that approach permits the story to claim.
- **Interpretation:** what explains the finding and what it means; notice limits or
  null results that materially change the story. This is working context, not a
  requirement to include a limitation in the copy.
- **Stakes:** who might think or act differently, and which implications are the
  authors' conclusions versus a clearly identified extrapolation.

Preserve percentage points versus percentages, denominators, and measured outcomes.
Do not turn engagement into welfare, intentions into behavior, or ratings into
quality. A null estimate is not proof of no effect. A theoretical prediction is not
an observed finding; an illustrative scenario is not a reported event. Attribution
such as “the study says” does not repair an exaggerated causal claim.

Quotations require exact source text and a locator. A paper quotation must be
identified as written in the paper, not dressed up as an interview. If quotes are
missing, use paraphrase; offer interview questions only when useful to the brief.

## Find the story, then write the opening

Select findings for their explanatory value: how well they answer the reader's
question, their substantive importance, strength of support, and distinctiveness.
Supporting results, differences across groups, or changes over time may illuminate
the question better than the paper's headline estimate. Give the central finding
prominence, use others to develop or complicate it, and draw out their combined meaning.

For a news story or feature, give its three entry points different jobs:
**headline: the interesting tension; standfirst: the finding and its stakes;
opening paragraphs: a concrete way into the problem and a bridge to the study.**
The standfirst should disclose the central insight. The lead should move the reader
into the story rather than paraphrase the standfirst. Use the annotated patterns
and worked examples in [Openings and language](guidance/openings-and-language.md)
to make these choices at sentence and paragraph level.

Privately consider two or three angles, then choose the one with the clearest
reader stakes and strongest support. Do not make the user review a planning form
before receiving a draft.

Useful angles include an unexpected cost, a tradeoff, a familiar choice with a
hidden mechanism, a reversal of a documented expectation, or a consequential
difference between groups. A descriptive or null result can be newsworthy without
pretending to overturn a consensus. For theory, make the problem vivid and explain
the assumptions that help readers understand the insight.

Build the opening around the paper's specific actors and action. Name the puzzle
and deliver a substantive finding early. Use concrete verbs, occasional questions,
contrast, analogy, and varied sentence lengths where they earn their place. An
analogy should illuminate one relationship, not smuggle in another causal claim.
Do not default to “We are delighted to announce,” literature gaps, contribution
lists, or an inventory of methods. A requested publication announcement can still
celebrate the milestone after giving readers a reason to care.

Organize around discoveries and their meaning. Each paragraph or post should
answer the reader's next question. Explain methods where they illuminate how a
discovery becomes possible or resolve that question; intelligible evidence supplies
credibility without a separate narrative about the researchers' rigor. Retain and
explain technical terms readers need; translate or omit labels that add no useful distinction.

## Revise at five levels

For a full story or substantive revision, apply [Prose editing](guidance/prose-editing.md)
across the whole draft, including the body and ending. For a small edit or short
post, use the relevant decisions without launching a full audit.

- **Words:** Preserve the exact outcome and scope; choose technical terms deliberately;
  expose actions with verbs; retain useful abstract nouns; make unfamiliar abbreviations
  earn their introduction through clarity and reuse.
- **Sentences:** Link new information to what readers can place, give the main point
  emphasis, align comparisons, use punctuation to clarify relationships, and attach
  qualifications to the claims they limit.
- **Length variation:** Let longer sentences integrate reasons, conditions, and
  comparisons. Use shorter ones to orient, answer, or mark a turn. Split by conceptual
  load rather than by a word quota.
- **Paragraphs:** Give each paragraph one governing message supported by as many
  distinct claims as it needs. Split at a new message, combine dependent fragments,
  and develop missing explanatory steps only with supported material.
- **Pacing:** Distribute new concepts and numbers, make transitions express actual
  relationships, place useful paragraph breaks, and repeat stable terms or examples
  when they provide continuity or a new payoff.

The [qualitative reference audit](audits/prose-audit.md) records weaknesses as well
as useful examples. These decisions are flexible editorial guidance, not universal
sentence-length rules or demonstrated engagement tactics.

## Adapt rather than truncate

Keep the finding and its evidentiary strength consistent across channels, but
rebuild the opening, order, emphasis, and ending for each audience. A LinkedIn
post can explore a professional decision; a university story can unpack a
mechanism; an X thread can reveal evidence one step at a time.

The numbers and quoted words remain stable. Do not add “first,” “breakthrough,”
“proves,” or predictions of sweeping change merely to raise the energy. Increase
specificity and narrative tension instead. Do not assume a formula, hashtag, or
link placement increases reach without relevant evidence.

## Deliver and check

Lead with finished copy, clearly separated by channel when there is more than one.
For a story include a headline and short standfirst; for a thread clearly separate
the posts. Supply alternatives only if requested or helpful to a real framing
choice. Follow requested file locations; otherwise respond in Markdown.

Use a verified paper link in the copy where it fits. If it is unavailable, say so
in a short editorial note and omit the link rather than inventing one. Keep source
locators, unresolved facts, and proposed interview work outside the publishable text.
For a long or multi-format package, append brief evidence notes for key claims.
For a single short post, surface only material limitations or verification gaps.

Before delivering, check:

1. **Interest:** a reader can identify the stakes and learn something concrete early.
   The standfirst gives an actual insight; the lead adds a situation, puzzle, or
   comparison. Each opening paragraph advances beyond the previous one.
2. **Faithfulness:** headline, hook, numbers, causal verbs, quotes, and takeaway agree
   with the source, including relevant null findings and scope.
3. **Genre:** this reads like the requested public format, with a coherent story and
   an ending that delivers on the opening, rather than a compressed manuscript.
   Concrete actions explain processes; useful abstract nouns hold concepts together.
   Specific consequences carry the drama.
   Remove interchangeable scene-setting, repeated qualifications, and commentary
   announcing that a finding is important instead of explaining its consequence.
4. **Usability:** credits and links are correct; short posts fit the requested budget
   including numbering and URLs. Label approximate counts honestly.
5. **Status:** a media-style draft carries no invented outlet byline, interview,
   endorsement, or claim that coverage has been secured.

This skill drafts and edits. Posting, scheduling, sending a pitch, or contacting
researchers requires a separate explicit instruction; do not infer it from a
request to prepare promotional copy.
