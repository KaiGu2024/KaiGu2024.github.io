# University news and media features

Choose whose story this is. University news explains and credits research in an
institutional voice. An author essay can interpret an idea in the first person.
Independent editorial coverage gives the reader distance from the authors' framing.
A pitch is an invitation to report, not a miniature press release.

## University news or magazine story

Default to a headline, a one- or two-sentence standfirst, and roughly 500–800 words
for a full story; respect the requested length and available evidence. Do not pad
an abstract-only brief to a magazine length with invented context or quotes.

Choose the structure that fits the finding:

| Story engine | Useful sequence | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| Chain of consequences | Specific change → affected actors → first result → downstream effects → broader design tradeoff | Platform policies, markets, interventions |
| Everyday choice | Familiar hypothetical → hidden constraint → research insight → conditions → return to the opening choice | Theory, decision research, mechanisms |
| Question under dispute | Public question → what was previously uncertain → decisive comparison → results → what remains unresolved | Experiments, contested claims |
| Revealing pattern | Concrete observation → scope of evidence → pattern and exceptions → interpretation → next question | Descriptive or qualitative research |

These are options, not instructions to force a causal chain onto correlational
evidence. When using an imagined scenario, signal it as such. With a real person
or event, use documented facts and preserve the original source.

Introduce authors, affiliation, paper, and publication status naturally when the
study enters the story. Explain a design through its comparison: what changed,
for whom, and compared with what. Use one or two memorable numbers with units and
baselines, rather than importing a regression table. Translate theory into a
decision under stated assumptions, not claims about what people demonstrably do.

Subheads should advance the argument. A closing image, return to the opening, or
specific unanswered question can provide a satisfying ending. Do not turn a
short-run experiment into a prediction of cumulative long-run effects. The
institution's name and publication prestige are credits, not the reason the
finding is true.

## Media-style feature

When asked for coverage “like the Economist” or “like the New York Times,” translate
that into broad editorial choices: analytic economy, human stakes, concrete scenes,
clear explanation, and reported scrutiny. Use supplied examples to calibrate
structure and tone, not to copy distinctive prose. Label the result as a draft;
do not create an outlet byline or imply publication or endorsement.

For roughly 600–1,000 words, a useful arc is **public problem or documented scene →
why it matters now → finding → how the study gets there → outside context or
counterevidence → limits → implication**. If no timely development is verified,
use the enduring question instead of manufacturing a news peg.

Earned-media coverage normally involves editorial selection and reporting beyond
the authors' promotional materials. Distinguish what can be drafted from what
requires reporting. A paper-only brief can produce a strong explanatory feature,
but cannot supply independent expert reactions or eyewitness scenes. Use a compact
editorial note to identify any reporting gaps, without filling the story with fake
interview placeholders.

When outside context is requested or materially needed, verify it from original
sources and mark differences in population, design, and timing. Additional press
releases repeating the same paper are not independent corroboration. Give an
opposing interpretation space only when the evidence warrants it; do not fabricate
balance. Keep the author's interpretation distinguishable from the article's
broader inference.

## Press pitch

Draft a subject line and roughly 120–200 words. Give the editor a concrete story:
the question, surprising and defensible finding, audience stakes, evidence, and a
paper link. Include a genuine news peg if available. Mention an embargo exactly
as provided; offer interview availability only when supplied. Tailor a pitch to a
named journalist using verified beat information if requested. Do not promise
coverage, invent exclusivity, or send the pitch without explicit instruction.

## Making prose more engaging

Replace an abstract noun with the actor doing something; explain a mechanism as a
sequence readers can follow; let a precise number resolve the opening puzzle.
Use a concrete comparison before technical labels. Vary pace: a short sentence can
mark a turn after a longer explanation. When an opening feels flat, change the
angle before reaching for stronger adjectives.

**Synthetic example:** A descriptive study finds that independent publishers'
participation fell after a platform introduced a fee; it cannot isolate the fee's
causal effect. “When free promotion acquired a price tag, the mix of publishers
changed” introduces a story the data can support. “A fee killed independent
publishing” invents both a causal conclusion and an industry-wide outcome. The
next paragraph should specify the platform, observed change, and study design.
