# Academic Voice — prose and journal adaptation

Use this guide for wording, sentences, paragraphs, and pacing; [main-text](main-text.md) covers substantive development. The defaults draw on the author's preferences, [writing sources](evidence/academic-voice-sources.md), and a [three-paper audit](evidence/academic-voice-three-paper-audit.md). The optional journal-voice pass below applies when the author requests adaptation to a journal.

## Choose words and subjects

Write for academics studying related questions. Retain familiar disciplinary terms and explain the process or comparison specific to this argument. Prefer the familiar, specific word when alternatives mean the same thing; retain technical language when it names the concept more accurately.

Express actions in verbs when this reveals who does what. Keep nominalizations that name an established concept or carry a useful topic forward. Replace vague evaluative adjectives and strings of abstract nouns with the action, relationship, or object they stand for.

Choose subjects by their role: actors for behavior, estimates or evidence for empirical patterns, prior researchers for attributed claims, and “I” or “we” for author actions and judgments. The author generally prefers natural impersonal subjects, while retaining first person where it makes agency clear. A passive is useful when the actor is irrelevant.

Repeat central terms to maintain continuity. Resolve vague pronouns and bare demonstratives when several antecedents are plausible. Introduce an abbreviation when repeated use earns the reader's learning effort. Preserve useful analogies, deliberate conversational turns, and individual voice. Judge wording and punctuation by their work in the passage.

## Build sentences around information and emphasis

Give the main clause a recoverable subject and verb. Inspect long interruptions between them, nested qualifications, ambiguous modifiers, noun stacks, and competing comparisons. A long parallel list may be easier to follow than a short sentence packed with unfamiliar abstractions.

Often begin with the relevant actor, established topic, or condition, then advance to new information. Put the intended emphasis in the main clause or near a natural point of closure. Move incidental procedure, parentheses, or citation clusters if they displace the finding.

Use parallel grammar for comparable groups, methods, or conditions. Coordination gives claims comparable weight; subordination marks a condition, concession, or explanation. Check which claim receives the main-clause emphasis and keep the scope of negation and restrictive words clear.

**Give each sentence one main message.** A claim and its closely connected reason can belong together. Split when another finding, comparison, mechanism, or implication needs its own attention. Resolve one unfamiliar relationship before introducing the next. Prefer simple, fairly short sentences when they carry the reasoning comfortably; readability takes priority over sentence length.

Lower information density by unpacking noun stacks into clauses and giving actors clear actions. Keep the small words that express the reasoning, such as "because," "so," and "for example." A revision may need more words to make an intermediate step explicit. Combine sentences when they repeat their setup or separate a relationship that readers can understand more easily together.

Let punctuation reveal hierarchy. A colon can unpack a statement; a semicolon can connect related independent clauses; parentheses can hold secondary material. Keep the main reasoning outside nested asides.

Choose tense according to what the sentence describes: a completed research action, a finding presented in the paper, an established relationship, or a future possibility. Preserve the intended timing through revisions.

## Develop paragraphs and control pacing

**Give each paragraph one governing point.** Its evidence, explanation, and interpretation should develop that point. Start a new paragraph when the argumentative task changes, such as moving from establishing a pattern to explaining a mechanism. One finding may need several paragraphs. Let the reader complete one step before beginning another; use the space needed to make each step clear.

Orient readers early to the paragraph's point and connection to the argument. Develop the evidence or explanation it needs. End where readers gain the intended understanding: a resolved comparison, an implication, or a question prepared by the reasoning. A summary sentence earns its place when it brings the preceding material together.

**Split or combine by function.** Split when the governing question changes, separate comparisons need their own explanation, or a completed step deserves a pause. Combine when adjacent paragraphs restart the same point or leave support detached from its claim. A long paragraph can be underdeveloped if it lists evidence without interpretation; a short paragraph can complete a narrow bridge. Sentence counts can flag awkward passages for inspection. Printed rows depend on layout and provide no useful drafting target.

Manage pace through information density as well as length. Give a difficult inference enough space and resolve one unfamiliar comparison before introducing several others. A simpler sentence after dense reasoning can let its implication register; a short orientation before it can prepare the reader. Retain repeated openings or parallel structures when they make comparisons easier to follow.

Make handoffs show why the next paragraph belongs here in the larger argument. Carry forward a relevant term, finding, or unresolved question, then establish what the next step will clarify. Choose a transition that expresses the actual relationship: contrast, elaboration, dependency, qualification, or implication. Introduce the purpose before a procedure when readers need that orientation.

For example, after evidence about product entry: "To understand what this expansion means for consumers, we next examine how much the new products are used." The transition identifies both the next comparison and its purpose. "Furthermore, we examine usage" gives less guidance. Use a short purpose clause, a substantive opening sentence, or a connection already prepared by the preceding paragraph. Vary the form with the reasoning; add an explicit signpost when it helps readers follow the turn.

Keep attribution close to the proposition it supports. Use author-centered citation when a study's action or disagreement matters and claim-centered citation when the relationship is foregrounded. Place a citation where its scope is clear.

Allow findings to recur with different jobs: an introduction preview, an evidence-bearing Results passage, and a conclusion about implications. Remove repetition that adds no new understanding. End a paragraph when its point is developed; a closing sentence should add an inference or useful synthesis. Read paragraph openings and endings together to check the progression.

## Preserve the evidence

Preserve numbers, comparisons, citations, and the meaning of claims while editing. Keep LaTeX commands, cross-references, and bibliographic keys intact. A prose-only edit should flag a substantive problem rather than silently change the finding.

Use direct language for findings. Reserve hedges for a specific uncertainty; remove stacked qualifiers that merely weaken the sentence. Prefer naming the uncertain step to spreading doubt across the whole paragraph. Follow [main-text's scope guidance](main-text.md#state-the-answer-directly) for when a qualification needs to appear or recur.

## Revision check

Before returning a substantial passage, check paragraph jobs and handoffs, then sentence information load, then wording. Keep this check internal unless the user asks for a critique.

- Can each sentence's main message be stated simply? Unpack competing messages and unfamiliar comparisons.
- Does every sentence in a paragraph develop its governing point? Give a new argumentative task its own paragraph.
- Can readers explain why each paragraph follows the previous one and how it advances the paper's question? Repair missing connections with substantive transitions.
- Does each qualification affect interpretation? Remove defenses against unspecified objections while retaining meaningful scope and uncertainty.
- Are claims, numbers, comparison groups, citations, and LaTeX commands preserved? Check especially that direct wording has kept the original evidentiary strength.

Read the revised passage once for flow. Keep passages that already work. Use the [worked examples](../guidance/prose-revision-examples.md) when density, paragraph structure, transitions, or defensive wording need repair. Fixed word counts and connective quotas are unnecessary; assess whether the reader can follow the reasoning.

## Optional journal-voice pass

Use the supplied journal, article type, section, and available exemplars to calibrate register and explanation density. Read relevant passages in context; verify official guidance when a submission requirement matters. Distinguish observed practices from requirements and keep source locators for recommendations attributed to a venue. If exemplars are unavailable, use the prose defaults and mention that limitation when it affects the requested adaptation.

Revise in paragraph context, preserving the author's argument and purposeful stylistic choices. By default, return a compact original/revised comparison of changed passages with brief reasons, followed by the clean text. Honor requests for clean text, critique, or tracked edits instead. For ordinary drafting, return the requested text with explanations only where they help the author assess a substantive choice.
