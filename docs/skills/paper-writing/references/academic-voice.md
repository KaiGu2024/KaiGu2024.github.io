# Academic Voice — calibrated prose and journal adaptation

Use the calibrated defaults below for academic prose within `paper-writing`. They combine the author's preferences, [writing scholarship and instructional sources](academic-voice-sources.md), and an [audit of three empirical papers](academic-voice-three-paper-audit.md). These are adaptable editorial principles, not verified journal requirements or experimentally established recipes. Read the sources or audit when checking a recommendation's basis; ordinary editing does not require repeating the research. [main-text](main-text.md) owns section structure and substantive development; this reference owns wording, sentences, paragraphs, pacing, and evidence-preserving revision.

**The separate journal-voice pass remains opt-in:** run it when explicitly requested or when the author asks to adapt finished prose to a journal. Naming a target venue during drafting sets the audience; it does not automatically request a separate voice report.

## Match the requested task

For a **draft**, build from the supplied facts and purpose using the relevant main-text guidance. For a **rewrite**, improve the prose within the authorized scope. For **critique**, identify consequential problems with local examples without replacing the text. For an **outline**, arrange claims and the evidence each requires without inventing findings. Keep proofreading narrow.

Infer the audience, genre, and editing scope from the request and material. Ask only when missing information would materially change the result. Keep papers, cover letters, and referee responses in their respective registers. These preferences do not expand the skill into a general correspondence workflow.

## Calibrated defaults

- **Write for academics studying related questions.** Retain familiar disciplinary terms; explain the comparison or mechanism specific to this argument. Familiar methods do not need textbook introductions. Keep formal detail where precision depends on it.
- **Compress while preserving inference.** Remove repeated setup, but retain the logical bridge the reader needs. Concision concerns useful work per word; the clearer revision may be longer.
- **Choose subjects by their argumentative role.** The author's preference for natural impersonal subjects is not a journal convention or a quota. Use relevant actors for behavior, estimates or evidence for empirical patterns, prior researchers for attributed claims, and “I” or “we” for author actions, findings, and interpretive judgments. Preserve a useful passive when the actor is irrelevant. The three exemplars mix these subjects; do not erase authorial agency merely to sound formal.
- **Make signposting earn its space.** Use a research question, preview, transition, or main-finding statement when it helps readers follow the argument. Scale it to the passage; do not require a headline number in every paragraph, equal numbers of questions and findings, or a triumphant ending.
- **Preserve purposeful individuality.** Allow a useful analogy, occasional conversational sentence, or dry aside. Do not manufacture personality, copy an exemplar's catchphrases, or flatten deliberate stylistic choices.
- **Edit for precision, function, and rhythm.** The author has supplied no blacklist of disliked words or punctuation. Evaluate qualifiers and transitions in context rather than treating them as automatic AI tells.

The current request overrides these defaults, including audience, register, authorial voice, length, and editing scope.

## Choose words for meaning and audience

Prefer the familiar, specific word when alternatives mean the same thing. Retain a longer technical term when it names the construct or method more accurately. Avoid ornamental sophistication, vague evaluative adjectives, and strings of abstract nouns that leave the action unclear. Express actions in verbs when this reveals who does what; keep nominalizations when they name an established concept or carry a useful topic forward. Neither short words nor active verbs are universal objectives.

Keep construct names and referents stable. Repeating a central term is useful continuity; substitute synonyms only when meaning remains exact. Resolve vague pronouns and bare demonstratives when several antecedents are plausible. Introduce an abbreviation when its subsequent use earns the reader's learning effort; avoid giving a rarely used concept a second name merely to shorten it.

Explain an unfamiliar technical idea through the process or contrast that gives it meaning, then use the precise term consistently. Formal voice does not require extra equations or jargon. A brief intuition or hypothetical illustration can help if clearly identified and consistent with the formal statement; never supply invented empirical evidence.

## Build sentences around information and emphasis

Give the main clause a recoverable subject and verb. Inspect long interruptions between them, nested qualifications, ambiguous modifiers, noun stacks, and competing comparisons. Word count alone does not measure difficulty: a long parallel list can be easier to follow than a short sentence containing several unfamiliar abstractions.

Often begin with the relevant actor, established topic, or condition, then advance to new information. Put the intended emphasis in the main clause or near a natural point of closure. Check that incidental procedure, a parenthesis, or a citation cluster has not displaced the finding. These are reader-orientation choices, not requirements that every sentence follow the same order or finish with its most important word. Preserve necessary qualifications beside the claims they limit.

Use parallel grammar for comparable groups, methods, or conditions, keeping the comparison base explicit. Coordination can give claims comparable weight; subordination can show that one is a condition, concession, or explanation of another. Do not accidentally subordinate the main finding or make a tentative explanation sound established. Keep the scope of negation, uncertainty, and restrictive words unambiguous.

**Long sentences integrate; short sentences focus.** A longer sentence can connect a result to its condition, contrast, or explanation without making the reader reconstruct their relationship. A shorter sentence can introduce a question, mark a turn, or crystallize an implication. Split when distinct emphases cannot be expressed clearly within the sentence or the reader must retain too many unresolved dependencies. Parallel clauses and internal punctuation can accommodate more than one emphasis. Combine when separate sentences repeat their setup or conceal a close logical relationship. Do not impose a word ceiling or alternate long and short mechanically.

Let punctuation reveal hierarchy. A colon can unpack a statement; a semicolon can connect closely related independent clauses; parentheses can hold genuinely secondary material. Avoid nesting asides or putting a qualification essential to validity where it looks dispensable. Dashes and other punctuation are contextual choices, not style violations.

## Develop paragraphs and control pacing

Privately identify each paragraph's governing message and argumentative job. One message may require several related claims, comparisons, or pieces of evidence. Use the section moves in [main-text](main-text.md); findings and interpretations can span multiple paragraphs. Do not equate one paragraph with one sentence, one result, or one introduction move.

Usually orient the reader early to the paragraph's point and connection to the argument. Develop the evidence, explanation, or qualification needed for that job. End where the reader has gained the intended understanding: a supported implication, resolved comparison, boundary, or question earned by the preceding reasoning. Do not append a redundant summary or require every ending to sound momentous. A roadmap, setting description, and findings paragraph need different kinds of development; a claim–evidence–analysis sequence is a useful diagnostic, not a mandatory template.

**Split or combine by function, not by rows.** Split when the governing question changes, separate comparisons need their own explanation, or a completed step deserves a pause. Combine when adjacent paragraphs merely restart the same point or leave supporting sentences detached from the claim. A long paragraph can still be underdeveloped if it lists evidence without interpretation; a short paragraph can be complete if it performs a narrow bridge or qualification. Sentence and word counts can flag unusual passages for inspection, but supply no automatic minimum, maximum, or ideal. Printed rows depend on column width, font, and layout. The three-paper audit describes variation; its observed ranges are not drafting targets.

Manage pace through information density as well as length. Give a difficult inference enough space; avoid introducing several unfamiliar concepts, comparisons, and qualifications before resolving any of them. A simpler sentence after a dense explanation can let its implication register; a short orientation before that explanation can prepare the reader. Repeated short sentences may fragment reasoning, while successive dense sentences may obscure hierarchy. Vary where needed, retaining repeated openings or parallel structure when they keep a comparison legible. Paragraph breaks can mark a shift in task or offer a useful pause; avoid uniformly sized blocks and one-line emphasis by habit.

Make sentence and paragraph handoffs express an actual relationship: qualification, contrast, elaboration, dependency, or implication. Carry forward a relevant term or finding before advancing. Choose a connective only after identifying that relationship; it cannot repair a missing inference. In empirical prose, distinguish a logical conclusion from a claim that one phenomenon causes another. Introduce an inferential obstacle or comparison before a procedure when its purpose would otherwise be obscure.

Interpret findings in relation to the question. Put the substantive comparison where readers can find it, attach decisive evidence and a table or figure locator, and explain what the contrast establishes when warranted. Orient readers to an unfamiliar display only as much as necessary. Use a meaningful magnitude or benchmark when supplied and useful; state both the size and its comparison base when needed. Distinguish per-item from aggregate effects, averages from tails, levels from changes, and percentages from percentage points.

Keep attribution close to the proposition it supports. Use author-centered citation when a particular study's action or disagreement matters; use claim-centered citation when the substantive relationship is foregrounded. Avoid citation placement that makes a source appear to support several distinct claims indiscriminately. Do not delete necessary references to improve cadence, or bury indispensable assumptions in footnotes; rearrange the prose while preserving support and scope.

Allow the same finding to recur at different levels of detail: a compressed introduction preview, an evidence-bearing results passage, and a conclusion about its implications can each serve a distinct purpose. Remove repetition within a passage that adds no new relation, evidence, or interpretation; do not remove cross-section continuity mechanically.

For a substantive prose revision, inspect paragraph jobs and handoffs first, sentence hierarchy and cadence next, and individual words last. Read openings and endings together to check progression, then read whole paragraphs to assess development and rhythm. Counts or readability scores can prompt inspection, but cannot certify clarity or justify replacing necessary technical terms. During a voice-only pass, flag an absent inferential link or unsupported interpretation outside the revised prose; supplying new reasoning belongs to a substantive revision. Keep proofreading within its narrower scope.

## Preserve the evidence

Keep claims, numbers, units, denominators, dates, comparison groups, citations, equation labels, and uncertainty attached to their original support. Preserve LaTeX commands, cross-references, and bibliographic keys. Retain central construct terms under main-text's construct lock.

Do not turn association into causation, a model implication into an observed effect, or an imprecise estimate into evidence of no effect. A changed estimand, omitted qualification, new interpretation, or reversed comparison is substantive. A voice-only pass must flag such problems rather than silently repair them. Do not replace an author's belief with “the data suggest” unless the original claim already has that evidentiary basis.

**Calibrate confidence at the claim.** State a supported finding directly; distinguish an interpretation consistent with it, a conjectured explanation, and an extrapolation beyond the observed setting. Attach uncertainty to the inference it qualifies rather than weakening every sentence. Author-centered language can help identify a judgment as such, but “we find” does not itself establish identification and “suggests” does not repair an unsupported claim. Preserve population, period, measurement, and design limits beside claims whose scope depends on them.

Missing evidence does not warrant decorative precision. Preserve an honest qualitative statement when sufficient. Identify an essential gap outside the proposed final prose; use marked placeholders only for a requested scaffold or unfinished draft. Never invent a citation or section reference to make a revision appear supported.

Explain what a limitation changes when substantive revision is authorized. A sensitivity check can weaken a conclusion and is not automatically reassurance; a bound requires assumptions that establish its direction. Include welfare, policy, or stakeholder implications only where relevant and supported. Source papers supply evidence about writing practices, not findings to insert into unrelated work.

## Optional journal-voice pass

1. **Establish the target and evidence.** Use the supplied journal, manuscript section, article type, and exemplars. If a venue-specific requirement matters, verify current official author guidance. Read comparable sections of available exemplars in full context; use more than one when feasible before describing a recurring practice. Ask for exemplars only when needed and unavailable through the project or accessible sources. If evidence is unavailable, apply the calibrated defaults and state that venue calibration is unverified.
2. **Separate requirements from preferences.** Distinguish official requirements, observed exemplar practices, and author preferences. Record source and section locators for any venue-specific recommendation. An observed choice in a few papers is not a universal journal rule. Do not assume a journal bans first person, requires a single tense, demands equations, or puts theorems before intuition. Choose tense according to what the sentence describes and preserve its temporal meaning. Match relevant register and explanation density without copying phrasing or adding technical content.
3. **Revise in paragraph context.** Keep, revise, or flag each passage. Check the reasoning first, then compression and cadence. Remove empty emphasis, unnecessary repetition, and vague wording only where the edit preserves meaning. Keep useful hedges, deliberate passives, and functional signposting. The goal is a clear version of the author's argument.
4. **Return a reviewable result.** By default, provide a compact original/revised comparison for changed passages with a brief reason tied to a calibrated default or sourced venue practice, followed by the clean revised text. Group related edits rather than narrating every punctuation change. If the user requests clean text only, critique only, or tracked edits, honor that format. Separate substantive flags from accepted voice edits and mention unverified venue assumptions only when material.

For ordinary drafting or revision, return the requested text with only useful explanations of material choices or unresolved evidence. Do not expose internal planning or routine self-review. A separate comparison report belongs to the requested journal-voice pass, not every paragraph edit.
