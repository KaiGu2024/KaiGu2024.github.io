# Introduction

Build a short path from the substantive question to the paper's answer and contribution. Start with the construct when readers need its relationship to another subject explained before the setting becomes informative. An existing mechanism, concrete puzzle, or the question itself can provide a faster opening when that relationship is already clear.

## Developing the first two or three paragraphs

Develop **conceptual relationship → concrete manifestation → consequential problem**, followed by a research-question bridge. Problematization can begin early and sharpen as the opening becomes more concrete.

1. **Establish the construct through its relationship to the paper's other central subject.** Choose the abstraction that carries the question: an outcome, explanatory factor, process, or relationship. Define its essential meaning where needed. Explain the historical, theoretical, practical, or substantive connection that motivates the inquiry. History earns space when it explains how the relationship arose or why a prior expectation makes sense. Readers should now see the direction of the question; specific sites, datasets, and measures can enter later.

2. **Narrow to a concrete manifestation and explain why it is informative.** Introduce a form, application, practice, or institution that realizes the abstraction. Describe development or adoption that changes the relationship, reveals a relevant condition, or raises the stakes. Explain why it helps investigate the same conceptual question. A more specific noun phrase can maintain continuity; the substantive narrowing should show what changed in scope and why it matters. The manifestation, study setting, and measure can enter at different points.

3. **Develop the consequential problem.** Identify what remains uncertain and why resolving it matters. Practical motivation names the decision, behavior, or consequence affected. Theoretical motivation identifies an assumption, mechanism, prediction, or boundary condition that existing explanations leave unresolved. Explain how an omitted moderator or mitigating process could change the account. Distinguish an unexamined application from a prediction contradicted by evidence. Establish the stakes, then use the bridge to articulate the precise question.

**Paragraph allocation.** In a two-paragraph motivation, establish the conceptual relationship and initial uncertainty in paragraph 1; connect its manifestation to the sharpened problem in paragraph 2. In a three-paragraph motivation, the manifestation and developed problem can each receive a paragraph. Alternatively, develop the theoretical problem before introducing the manifestation when that order makes the setting's relevance clearer. Signal the question's direction early; do not spend two paragraphs on background before revealing what needs explaining. Follow with the research-question bridge, or let the final motivation paragraph perform that function when it can do so clearly without repetition.

## Research-question bridge

Connect the final motivating problem to the nuance of the closest prior research: **what prior work establishes → what its assumptions, scope, or evidence leave unresolved → the question this paper must answer empirically**. Use a focused paragraph before "what we do" when the gap needs its own development. Integrate the bridge into the final motivation paragraph or the opening of the study preview when the reasoning is already compact and clear. Cite only the work needed to establish the gap; broader literature positioning follows the findings.

Explain why available knowledge does not settle the relevant direction, magnitude, conditions, or interpretation. "Therefore, this is an empirical question" should express that reasoning. A known directional prediction can still leave important magnitudes or boundary conditions unresolved. State the question as an interrogative or research objective using the constructs established in the opening. Show how any subquestions develop it.

**Where to explain answerability.** If overcoming an observation, measurement, or identification obstacle is central, explain the specific capability gained. Put this in the bridge when it establishes the question, or in "what we do" when it needs the dataset or design description. Develop it once.

## From the research question to the contribution

Use the preferred progression **what we do → findings and their interpretation → literature positioning → contributions and their significance → roadmap**. Findings and interpretation can develop together or across several paragraphs.

- **What we do.** Explain how the empirical approach answers the question. Introduce the setting, data, comparison, and design at the depth needed to understand the answer. Explain the source of variation and why the comparison is informative. Include a measurement detail when readers would otherwise misunderstand the conceptual claim; leave implementation for later sections.
- **Findings and interpretation: develop a logical line.** Identify the central answer and select supporting findings by their [role in the argument](abstract.md#select-evidence-by-its-job). Order them by what readers must understand first, explaining how each finding develops the answer. State the substantive finding with a selective magnitude, then connect the combined answer to the opening relationship. If an alternative-explanation analysis distinguishes competing interpretations, integrate its question and answer here.
- **Literature positioning.** Use a focused paragraph to locate the paper within the relevant literature strands: what the closest work establishes, how the strands connect, and where this paper extends, qualifies, or links them. Establish the scholarly relationship rather than repeat the motivating gap or list citations. Expand across paragraphs when meaningful comparisons require it.
- **Contributions and their significance.** A separate paragraph can explain what the paper adds. For substantive empirical papers, build this primarily from the findings: what they change about the phenomenon, explanation, or decision. Literature positioning locates the work; this paragraph develops the value of its answer. A methodological advance changes research capability; a theoretical advance changes an explanation, prediction, or boundary condition. Explain the contribution the paper actually makes and what it changes for relevant actors or fields.
- **Roadmap.** End the introduction with a short paragraph describing the actual order and purpose of the remaining sections. Match the manuscript's section names and numbering; avoid repeating the findings or contributions.

**Optional design and mechanism paragraphs.** Give a distinctive design or mechanism its own paragraph when understanding that contribution needs more space. Otherwise integrate design into "what we do" and interpretation into "what we find."
