# Abstract

## Compress the argument, not the paper

Treat the abstract as the paper's intellectual argument in miniature, not one sentence per manuscript section. Finalize it after the findings and framing stabilize; an earlier working abstract can help clarify the argument.

Use the author's word budget. For submission-ready work, check the journal's official limit and format. Otherwise draft to the space needed for a concise, self-contained argument.

Develop the functions below across the available space, combining or reordering them as needed; end with a significance sentence. Allocate detail to the contribution: a measurement comparison may need method and benchmark detail, while a substantive tension may need only a brief design description.

1. **Problem and tension.** Establish the question, puzzle, or decision. Name the central construct—the abstraction represented by the empirical objects—and its relationship to the paper's other subject when readers need that framing. Explain what remains unresolved and why it matters. Open with the paper's specific problem; a literature shortage or a technology's growing importance rarely establishes it by itself.
2. **Empirical object.** Connect the question to the setting, comparison, and design. Give readers enough to understand what the evidence represents. Describe a data or methodological advantage when central to the contribution; otherwise use the space for the substantive argument. Name methods when their comparison is the question and compress implementation detail elsewhere.
3. **Central finding or connected findings.** State the answer, using magnitudes when they make its size or comparison clearer. Connect multiple findings through their substantive relationship: direct response and wider consequence, benefit and offsetting cost, or relative improvement and remaining inadequacy. Give numbers their necessary units and benchmarks. Organize around what the results jointly establish.
4. **Interpretation and informative extensions.** Include a mechanism, boundary condition, institutional comparison, or simulation if it explains or changes the headline answer. Otherwise move directly to significance.
5. **Closing significance statement.** End with a sentence explaining what the findings change in understanding, theory, evaluation, or a relevant actor's decisions. Connect this consequence to the opening problem. Replace generic claims of new insights or important implications with the specific change the paper establishes.

The abstract should stand alone: explain an unfamiliar central construct or comparison enough to understand the answer. The [introduction](introduction.md) develops the conceptual argument and literature positioning; [Results](results.md) provides the detailed evidence.

## Select evidence by its job

Select findings by their role in the paper's argument:

| Role | Abstract treatment |
|---|---|
| Headline | State the central finding or decomposition directly. |
| Explanatory | Explain why, where, or for whom the headline holds. |
| Interpretive extension | Include selectively if it changes the theoretical or managerial takeaway. |
| Corroborative | Usually fold into the headline clause. |
| Robustness | Usually omit from the abstract; retain when it materially qualifies credibility or is itself the contribution. |

Assign roles by the paper's claim. A simulation can be the headline in a model-based paper; validation can be central in a measurement paper. Use a deletion test: if removing an analysis leaves the central interpretation unchanged, it probably does not need its own abstract sentence. Evidence that changes the answer belongs in the account.

## When components change the aggregate's interpretation

When opposing components are the contribution, foreground them and explain the net outcome. When the aggregate is the central question, lead with it and use components to explain its size. [Results](results.md#make-magnitudes-and-comparisons-interpretable) covers the accounting and comparison details.
