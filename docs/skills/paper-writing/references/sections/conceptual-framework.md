# Conceptual Framework

## Purpose

For a substantive empirical paper whose theoretical contribution is modest, the primary job is conceptual grounding: introduce the major constructs as existing literatures understand them, explain their established or debated relationships, and clarify the perspective the study adopts. This synthesis can be the section's main contribution to reader understanding. Scale further theoretical development to the paper's question and contribution.

A construct is a high-level abstraction of the empirical objects or processes studied. Theory explains relationships among constructs: why a relationship should arise, under which assumptions, and within what scope. The section can draw on existing explanations without proposing a new theory. Where prior work establishes a pattern but leaves its explanation unsettled, preserve that distinction.

## Ground the account before drafting

Work from the supplied manuscript, research notes, and relevant source passages. Identify the framework's purpose: causal explanation, descriptive or predictive organization, measurement, formal theory, or a taxonomy of domains. Preserve complementary frameworks when they answer different questions. A domain map need not become a causal chain, and a conceptual argument need not have an operational measure for every idea.

For each central construct or relationship, establish its meaning, role, source, and evidence status. When these are difficult to track, use a compact internal map: **element → definition or claim → relationship type → measure or manipulation, if any → evidence status → source locator**. Read the methods, measures, or supplements when a retained claim depends on them; identify missing support rather than filling it in. This map supports drafting and need not appear in the manuscript or become a separate deliverable.

Distinguish what prior authors propose, what their evidence supports, what the current study proposes or tests, and what the writer reconstructs or extends. Cite the passage that supports the particular definition or relationship. Label an interpretive synthesis where attribution could otherwise imply that a source made the same argument. Preserve unresolved source gaps with specific placeholders in an unfinished draft.

## Core development: constructs, relationships, and the study's perspective

### Introduce the major constructs through existing literature

Introduce the constructs readers need beyond the introduction's brief framing. Explain how relevant literatures define them, which dimensions or neighboring concepts matter, and which meaning the study uses. Develop distinctions that change how readers understand the question; define familiar constructs briefly.

State whose behavior, belief, or outcome is involved and at what level: individual, relationship, organization, or market. Distinguish the concept from the intervention that may change it and the indicator used to observe it. Include the relevant time horizon when immediate and longer-run responses may differ.

Explain the conceptual meaning of the empirical representation when it affects the argument. A manipulation, a check that the manipulation worked, and a measure of a proposed mediator have different roles. Mark a consequential mechanism as unmeasured when appropriate. Keep latent versus directly measured status separate from randomized versus nonrandomized assignment; observational research can model latent constructs. Develop reflective or formative measurement assumptions here only when they are part of the conceptual argument; leave scale items and construction details to Methods.

### Explain how the literature relates these constructs

Synthesize what prior theory proposes and empirical research has found about the focal relationships. Explain the reasoning behind those relationships and the conditions that affect their interpretation. Organize by constructs and relationships, with individual studies supporting the account. A coherent synthesis is sufficient when the study mainly contributes substantive evidence.

Bring literature perspectives together by explaining their different meanings, complementary relationships, or disagreements. Compare outcomes, actors, and assumptions to show whether an apparent disagreement concerns the same relationship. Develop the perspectives needed for this question.

Name the relationship precisely: proposed causal influence, prediction, association, measurement, or domain membership. A regression path or covariance does not establish a causal process. If feedback is part of the account, explain the sequence and time horizon of the reciprocal influences. Keep theoretical direction separate from the evidence that could identify that direction.

### Clarify the perspective used in this study

Explain which definitions and relationships organize the investigation and how the empirical setting instantiates them. Include institutional features when they clarify that connection. This can be a short synthesis of the preceding literature rather than a separate claim of theoretical novelty.

Close an early framework by connecting this understanding to the empirical question: what relationship, aspect, or condition the study examines and what remains to be learned.

State the essential empirical boundary when it changes that question: what is manipulated or observed, which comparison the study supports, and which proposed relationships remain untested. An identified treatment effect does not automatically identify every link in the framework. Methods supplies the identification argument and its assumptions; naming a regression, fixed effect, instrument, or discontinuity design is insufficient on its own.

## Further development when the argument needs it

Develop the following when they help explain the question or interpret its answer:

- **Mechanisms and conditions.** Develop the process linking constructs through incentives, information, beliefs, constraints, or interactions. Separate a mechanism, which explains how a relationship arises, from a moderator, which changes when or how strongly it operates. Explain why the proposed condition matters. When moving between actors or levels, explain the connection rather than inferring an aggregate consequence from an individual response alone.
- **Competing channels or explanations.** Show whether channels reinforce, offset, or depend on one another. Explain the basis for a directional prediction, or the reason its direction remains an empirical question. When explanations imply the same headline pattern, describe observations that could distinguish them.
- **Hypotheses or propositions.** Use them when distinct, justified predictions help organize the study. Derive their comparison and scope in the preceding prose. Numbering is optional; research questions can express genuine indeterminacy.
- **Formalization.** A model helps when assumptions, interdependence, aggregation, or welfare interpretation require greater precision. Explain its intuition and what its conclusions depend on; place extended derivations elsewhere when they interrupt the main argument.

For a methodological paper, develop the inferential problem and benchmark. For a framework that interprets results, establish the interpretation problem, develop the account, and connect it to evidence.

### Keep explanatory roles and support distinct

Use these distinctions when the argument involves the corresponding roles:

- **Mechanism and mediation.** A mechanism explains a process; a mediator represents an intermediate variable in a causal pathway. A total treatment effect, a manipulation check, or a pattern consistent with a mechanism does not by itself establish mediation. Explain what evidence distinguishes the proposed process from alternatives, or retain it as a conjectured explanation.
- **Moderation and boundary conditions.** Explain why a condition changes the focal relationship. Subgroup treatment effects under suitable design assumptions can establish heterogeneity without identifying the effect of changing an observed moderator. Prior timing or nonrandom assignment does not make a moderator a confounder.
- **Common causes and controls.** A confounder in the common-cause case influences both exposure and outcome. A control is a variable conditioned on in the analysis; association with both variables or inclusion in a regression is insufficient to establish confounding. A mediator or collider has a different role, and adjusting for it can change or bias the target comparison. Include these roles in conceptual development only when they explain the focal argument or an alternative account.

Distinguish theoretical uncertainty, imperfect measurement, and limits of identification where each matters. Match verbs to support: a relationship may be proposed, predicted, associated, or supported as causal under stated assumptions. Statistical significance or SEM fit alone does not settle that choice. Keep advance expectations, empirical findings, and explanations developed while interpreting findings distinguishable; exposition order does not establish research chronology.

## When a figure accompanies the prose

A figure represents the argument's structure; the prose supplies definitions, reasoning, assumptions, and scope. Keep construct names, relationship types, and evidence status consistent across both. A directional arrow can represent a hypothesis without claiming that its effect is identified. Distinguish covariance, unobserved common causes, feedback, and moderation; give each connector an explicit meaning. Keep measurement correspondence visibly separate from substantive relationships, and use noncausal grouping for domains or criteria.

## Boundaries with other sections and skills

The [introduction](introduction.md) previews the question and positions the paper's contribution. Here, literature positioning explains the meaning of the constructs and the logic of the theoretical account in enough depth to support the investigation. Avoid repeating the introduction's contribution claims.

[Data and Methods](data-and-methods.md) explains how concepts are observed and what the design identifies. Signal that connection here and leave operational details there. Results and Discussion assess the evidence and its implications.

Use a separate section when readers need sustained conceptual development. Institutional background can precede or accompany it when the reasoning depends on how the setting operates. Integrate a shorter account with the introduction, background, or analysis. Place a framework for interpreting findings where readers encounter that interpretation.

Before returning the section, check the strongest causal or mechanism claim against its supporting source and empirical comparison, and check that the construct definitions survive the handoff to measurement. Report unresolved issues that materially affect the argument; for a prose-only revision, flag a substantive problem rather than silently changing the finding.
