# Sources for academic-voice principles

Researched 2026-09-23 for [academic-voice.md](../academic-voice.md). This is a focused source review, not a systematic literature review or a set of journal requirements. We searched for scientific-writing principles, paragraph development, transitions, sentence variety, concision, and empirical research on lexical complexity/readability. We read original guidance from its publisher or university and original research, rather than relying on summaries. The [three-paper audit](academic-voice-three-paper-audit.md) separately checks how these principles appear in the selected empirical manuscripts.

## Instructional and conceptual sources

These sources recommend practices and explain revisions; they do not experimentally establish an optimum sentence or paragraph length.

| Source and locator | Principle used | Boundary on its use |
|---|---|---|
| George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan (1990), “The Science of Scientific Writing,” *American Scientist* 78:550–558. [Full text, reformatted PDF](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~pel/misc/gopen_swan.pdf), sections “Subject-Verb Separation,” “The Stress Position,” “The Topic Position,” “Perceiving Logical Gaps,” and “Locating the Action.” | Keep grammatical dependencies recoverable; orient with context; place consequential information at a natural point of emphasis; use verbs to clarify action. Structure can reveal an inferential gap. | The essay explicitly treats its recommendations as flexible and rejects fixed word limits. Information placement cannot be reduced to a sentence template. Revealing a gap does not authorize a voice editor to invent the missing argument. |
| Harvard College Writing Center, [“Anatomy of a Body Paragraph”](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/anatomy-body-paragraph), opening discussion and advice on paragraph endings. | A paragraph develops a main point through appropriate support and interpretation. Its ending can articulate what the reader can now understand. | The example is a humanities essay. Transfer the functional principle, without requiring setting descriptions, roadmaps, or empirical-study paragraphs to reproduce its three-part anatomy. |
| Harvard College Writing Center, [“Transitions”](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/transitions), opening discussion of familiar/new information and concluding revision advice. | Establish the relationship between adjacent ideas before selecting a connective. Repeated key terms can carry that relationship. | A connector does not establish missing logic, and logical implication does not by itself establish empirical causation. |
| Purdue OWL, [“Strategies for Variation”](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/index.html), introduction and sections 1–2. | Sentence length, structure, and openings can vary to manage rhythm and emphasis. | Treat variation as a repertoire. Mandatory alternation would undermine useful parallelism and topic continuity; the guide supplies no tested ideal ratio. |
| Purdue OWL, [“Concision”](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/index.html), opening and sections 1–3. | Replace vague circumlocution with effective wording; eliminate material without a useful function; combine closely related material where helpful. | A shorter substitution may change connotation or claim strength. Preserve inference, uncertainty, scope, and attribution even when doing so requires more words. |
| Purdue OWL, [“On Paragraphs”](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html), “When to start a new paragraph,” and [“Paragraphing”](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/paragraphing.html). | Paragraph breaks can mark a new point or give readers a pause. | The latter guide also offers a breakable beginner sentence-count heuristic. We do not claim that all writing authorities reject counts. Our choice is to use them diagnostically for advanced empirical prose. |

## Empirical studies and what they establish

**Daniel M. Oppenheimer (2006).** “Consequences of erudite vernacular utilized irrespective of necessity: problems with using long words needlessly.” *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20:139–156. [DOI/publisher](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1178); [full PDF](https://cahill.people.unm.edu/480-21/Oppenheimer-2006-Applied_Cognitive_Psychology.pdf), experiments 1–3 and general discussion, especially p. 152. Experiments with undergraduate readers evaluating manipulated texts support avoiding needless vocabulary complexity as a strategy for appearing intelligent. They do not show that shorter words improve expert comprehension or publication prospects in these fields. The discussion explicitly allows longer words when precision warrants them and recognizes expertise/jargon as a scope limitation. Use audience-appropriate precision, not a shortest-word rule.

**Pontus Plavén-Sigray, Granville James Matheson, Björn Christian Schiffler, and William Hedley Thompson (2017).** “The readability of scientific texts is decreasing over time.” *eLife* 6:e27725. [Original article](https://elifesciences.org/articles/27725), results and discussion. Their corpus analysis measures historical changes in scientific abstracts with readability formulas and examines general scientific jargon. It is observational, not an intervention establishing which edits improve understanding. The discussion notes that readability metrics omit features including rhetorical structure and coherence. Use length and lexical metrics as prompts for close reading; do not optimize a score at the expense of the argument. Its general, biomedical, and life-science corpus does not establish economics, marketing, or IS house style.

Both research articles' titles, authors, issue years, venues, and DOIs were checked against Crossref on the research date. Oppenheimer's PDF records an earlier online date than its 2006 issue year. The instructional pages are attributed to their institutions without invented publication dates.

## Editorial synthesis and allocation

The operational guidance combines these sources with the audit and the author's preferences. Added guidance on abbreviation burden, punctuation hierarchy, negation, and citation placement is an editorial application of clarity and evidence preservation, not a separately tested result. Paragraph pacing likewise concerns how much unresolved reasoning a reader must carry; the sources do not supply an optimal numerical formula.

Keep the division of responsibility clear: `main-text.md` specifies section purposes and substantive moves; `academic-voice.md` develops the words, sentences, and paragraphs that perform those moves; this source note records the basis and limits. The empirical-paper audit contains observations and counterexamples, not a store of sentences or topics to transplant into new papers.
