# Journal and department calibration

Use when a venue is specified or the author asks for a particular editorial style.
These are editorial lenses, not submission rules, estimates of acceptance, or a
claim that a small published sample establishes a journal's house style.

## 1. Calibrate the conversation, not the punctuation

A title should help the intended reader recognize a consequential contribution.
Different readers may recognize different objects in the same paper. Preserve the
evidence while deciding which object should lead.

Do not translate "Management Science style" into mandatory tradeoffs, "QJE style"
into maximal brevity, or "Marketing Science style" into an estimator-first title.
Do not infer an editor's personal preferences from authorship, a department label,
one accepted paper, or the model's familiarity with famous titles.

Separate three claims:

**Official rule or scope:** verified against a dated publisher or society source.
**Observed example:** a particular published title with its contribution and venue.
**Editorial inference:** why a similar structure might suit the present manuscript.

Only the first is a statement of journal policy. The third remains a judgment.

## 2. Management Science Information Systems

The official departmental statement covers the design, adoption, use, and impacts
of digital technologies with managerial and theoretical implications. It accepts
a range of methods; its predictive-analytics interest combines methodological
advance with a substantive managerial application. Source: the Management Science
[editorial statement](https://pubsonline.informs.org/page/mnsc/editorial-statement), checked 2026-09-15.

Translate this into questions about the manuscript, not compulsory title words:

| Positioning route | What the title can foreground | What the paper must actually contribute |
|---|---|---|
| Technology-enabled process | Advice, delegation, search, content production, coordination | A consequential process or outcome, not the mere presence of technology. |
| Incentives and participation | Trust, entry, contribution, disintermediation | The relevant relationship and actors. |
| Platform or organizational design | A design choice and the outcome it shapes | An analyzed choice, intervention, or decision problem. |
| Evaluation | A distinction that changes how success is measured | A consequential measurement or substantive evaluation argument. |
| Human–technology interaction | The division of work, discretion, or complementarity | Evidence or theory of the relationship, not an assumed mechanism. |
| Distribution and system consequences | Who benefits; when local and aggregate outcomes diverge | Appropriate outcomes and a justified level of analysis. |

A governance-relevant paper need not estimate an optimal policy. However,
"Optimizing Governance" or "An Effective Governance Intervention" promises more
than implications in the Discussion. "Evaluating," a named design relationship,
or an informative phenomenon-led title may match the evidence better.

Do not add "AI" to a traditional automation study merely because it is topical.
Do not label a translation workflow "generative AI" when that changes the perceived
treatment. Broader AI framing is possible when the technology classification and
workflow analogy are accurate and the concrete empirical object remains clear.

Do not hide a platform name automatically. It can make the evidence interpretable
and memorable. Conversely, do not let the platform name replace the question.
The useful comparison is often a setting-led title versus a general relationship
with the setting in the subtitle.

### Verify the department

Confirmed IS examples in the source record include the trust/disintermediation,
information-seeding, recommender-fragmentation, and platform-entry-cost papers.
Their publisher records explicitly identify information systems in the acceptance
information. They illustrate different structures, not a mandatory formula.

The long-term-targeting example was accepted through marketing. The recent
adoption-survey example was accepted through business strategy. Both can be useful
craft comparisons, but neither is direct evidence about IS-department style.
Special-issue acceptance should also be labeled as such rather than silently
converted into an ordinary department assignment.

## 3. Other audiences

These are editorial heuristics, not verified journal-specific title requirements:

| Audience | Recognition test | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| AER / QJE | Can an economist outside the narrow specialty identify the economic object, puzzle, or new quantity? | Inflating one setting into an economy-wide law or forcing every paper into a dramatic reversal. |
| Marketing Science | Can readers identify the consumer, firm, market, measurement, or decision contribution? | Calling behavior welfare, revenue profit, prediction targeting, or a routine tool a methods contribution. |
| Organization/management audiences | Is the organizational process, explanation, or conceptual distinction recognizable? | Adding abstract constructs not developed by the paper. |
| Methods-oriented readership | Is the problem, target quantity, or constraint clear? | Listing an algorithm's name without explaining the analytical task it changes. |

Match paper type as well as venue. A theory title, a field experiment title, a
measurement paper title, and a qualitative-process title need not have the same
syntax within one journal.

## 4. Assemble a comparable reference set

For an explicit deep calibration with browsing available:

1. Start from the journal's official scope and the closest research conversation.
   Search public topic terms; do not upload unpublished abstracts to external sites.
2. Inspect a small purposive set, often six to ten relevant papers when available.
   Prefer similar contribution types and recent papers; include an older exemplar
   only when its rhetorical function is useful. The count is not a requirement.
3. Read each abstract, and relevant methods or results when the title's promise
   cannot otherwise be assessed. Verify the exact title and publication version.
4. For MS, record the department or special issue from the acceptance statement
   when available. Do not infer it from the authors or keywords.
5. Include straightforward titles as well as questions and contrasts. Search for
   the subject, not only for "paradox" or "tradeoff," which would bias the examples.
6. Note what each title foregrounds, what it leaves to the abstract, and what
   evidence licenses its strongest word. Then draft without copying its wording.

A useful record is: exact title; source; journal; issue year; online-first date;
verified department if applicable; contribution type; title function; scope or
claim boundary. Citation counts and awards are optional contextual metadata,
not evidence of a wording advantage. Usually omit them.

The reference set is illustrative. Do not report a style frequency or universal
preference from it. If a full comparison cannot be verified, say what was checked
instead of manufacturing current journal rules.

## 5. Report the choice without manufactured authority

Prefer: "This version leads with the incentive conflict and keeps the platform
setting explicit, as in the verified IS examples."

Avoid: "Management Science editors prefer this formula" or "This title is most
likely to be accepted."

Use a direct phrase or exact-title search when the author requests a distinctiveness
check. Separate a generic shared term from a close match to an established paper.
A negative search is not proof of uniqueness. A memorable title can be distinctive
in meaning without requiring an unprecedented sequence of words.
