# Simple language and intellectual reach

Use when the author values simple language with a substantial intellectual world
behind it, or when accurate candidates feel like compressed abstracts. This guide
integrates the author's 2026-10-08 notes in the repository at
notes/paper-titles/notes.md. It is self-contained; that notes file is provenance,
not a runtime dependency.

The craft readings below are editorial interpretations, not established author
intentions or evidence of effects on readership, citations, or acceptance.

## From an analysis to a subject worth understanding

Know the finding, insight, and stakes internally. Then ask which part should meet
the reader first: the subject, the question, or the answer. The title need not
expose the whole argument. A recognizable human practice or consequential subject
can identify a worthwhile inquiry before its explanation is known.

Try a few language routes, using only those earned by the paper:

| Route | What to look for | What makes it substantive |
|---|---|---|
| Human action or scene | What people do, want, remember, pay for, or endure. | The practice opens the paper's central question. |
| Conceptual connection | Familiar things whose combination deserves attention. | Their relationship matters; the words are not a keyword list. |
| Consequential subject | An institution, event, or condition worth understanding. | Importance and focus sustain interest without ornament. |
| Reclassification | A familiar thing understood through another familiar category. | The paper develops the changed interpretation. |
| Earned double meaning | An expression whose literal and figurative senses both fit. | Both meanings help express the explanation or phenomenon. |
| Direct argument | An answer that intervenes in a recognizable question or debate. | The evidence supports the assertion; curiosity concerns why it is true. |

Work in both directions between ordinary language and scholarly concepts. A
concrete verb can be more exact than an abstract noun: persistence, for example,
may reside in "stay." Conversely, an established technical term may identify a
methods contribution more efficiently than a scene. Simplicity is access to
meaning, not compulsory avoidance of specialist vocabulary.

## Clear language with an open explanation

Aim for language understood immediately but not immediately exhausted. A reader
can recognize prayer, prejudice, or waiting while still wanting an explanation.
Productive incompleteness leaves the intellectual relationship open. Empty
vagueness leaves the reader unable to identify what the paper concerns.

For each promising short candidate, compare it with a more explicit version.
Ask what the extra words change. Add a subtitle when it supplies useful recognition,
an essential boundary, or the actual technical task; do not append the question,
setting, design, mechanism, and result by habit. Search terms can also appear in
the abstract and keywords. Follow explicit journal and user requirements.

Use a two-stage reading:
before the abstract, what intelligible inquiry does the title invite?
After the abstract, does the wording become apt or richer in meaning?
The title can reward reading without allowing prediction of the estimator or result.
If the explanation has to retract the title's first implication, revise it.

Broad subjects require judgment about the whole promise. A review spanning a
phenomenon may earn a title that would misrepresent a narrow outcome in one site.
No successful published example grants another paper the same breadth.

## Published examples

Records and abstracts checked on **2026-10-08** using primary publisher,
institutional, or author-hosted sources. Footnote markers are omitted. The first
three are the author's anchor examples; the others come from the supplied notes.
Versions are identified below rather than treating every example as a recent
journal publication. NBER's war record and Cairn's burial record were available
through indexed primary-source text; direct page retrieval failed, so no full-text
review is claimed for those two examples.

### Myths, Morals, and Movies

Stelios Michalopoulos and Christopher Rauh. Cambridge Working Papers in Economics,
**2026 repository revision**.
[Institutional record and abstract](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/e5ff6de9-0b09-43b7-918e-a12c96e00acd).

The paper connects movie appeal with local folklore and cultural values.
The wording moves from inherited stories through values to everyday entertainment.
Its conceptual connection gives the alliteration something to carry. "Movies"
makes the larger cultural question tangible. Borrow the meaningful connection,
not a requirement for three nouns or matching initials.

### Praying for Rain

José-Antonio Espín-Sánchez, Salvador Gil-Guirado, and Nicholas Ryan.
*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, **2026**.
[Publisher record and abstract](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/141/3/2363/8676046).

The paper studies when rainfall patterns make rainmaking rituals persuasive.
The title offers a human scene: people seeking influence over something beyond
their control. Its placement in economics invites an explanation without spelling
out the model or findings. It names the practice; it does not assert that prayer
causes rain. Learn how a scene opens an inquiry, not how to withhold essential meaning.

### The Economic Consequences of War

Efraim Benmelech and Joao Monteiro. **NBER Working Paper 34389, 2025**.
[Working-paper record and abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34389).

The paper examines war's macroeconomic aftermath, including real activity and
government finances. The title relies on the subject's importance and an organizing
question, without wordplay or a stated result. "Consequences" directs attention to
what follows. Its resemblance to Keynes's
[*The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919)](https://www.marshall.econ.cam.ac.uk/archives/economic-consequences-of-the-peace)
offers a possible historical resonance; that is a reading of the wording, not a
verified intentional allusion. Nor does the title promise every consequence of every war.

### Further examples from the notes

| [Stories, Statistics, and Memory](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/139/4/2181/7691253) — Thomas Graeber, Christopher Roth, and Florian Zimmermann; *QJE*, 2024. | Experiments compare how stories and statistics persist in memory and beliefs. The third noun supplies the dimension linking the first two; the triad organizes an inquiry without stating its result. |
| [Morals and Markets](https://polit.econ.kit.edu/downloads/papers/MoralsMarkets_Science.pdf) — Armin Falk and Nora Szech; *Science*, 2013, author-hosted published copy. | Experiments examine market interaction and willingness to accept harm to a third party. "Price" makes a moral attitude economically measurable; it names an actual opportunity cost rather than decorating a discrimination study. |
| [Burying the Hatchet](https://shs.cairn.info/revue-d-economie-politique-2020-6-page-1025?lang=en&tab=texte-integral) — Colin Harris and Adam Kaiser; *Revue d'économie politique*, 2020. | Proposes that burying possessions with the dead limits inheritance conflict. "Not" turns familiar behavior into a puzzle without naming a behavioral model. The wording does not mean every member never attends. |

The sentence performs a conceptual reclassification. It does not establish that all fines always increase violations. |

A spatial word makes an abstract domain accessible. A broad review supports this organizing territory; two evocative words alone do not establish scope. |
| [Why Do People Stay Poor?](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/137/2/785/6455333) — Clare Balboni, Oriana Bandiera, Robin Burgess, Maitreesh Ghatak, and Anton Heil; *QJE*, 2022. | Uses asset transfers and long-run household data in Bangladesh to study poverty traps. "Stay" makes persistence the question. Ordinary language carries an analytical distinction without claiming to explain all poverty. |

Stating the answer can create interest in the evidence; do not add the same argumentative emphasis to an unsupported result. |

## Apply the lessons without copying the surface

The shared virtues are conceptual compression, human significance, and restraint.
They do not imply that every title needs an idiom, a moral theme, a grand subject,
or a short word count. Historical resonance and idioms may not travel across
languages; the substantive inquiry should remain accessible without recognizing
the allusion.

Find one principal promise and compare an ordinary expression, a more explicit
version, and a technical version where useful. Preserve whichever gives the
intended reader the best access to the paper. Do not penalize a strong subject
title for leaving out its answer, or a strong answer title for leaving no mystery.
